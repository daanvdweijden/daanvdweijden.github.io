# The Contemporary Scientist

## Scientists are the new Creatives [^4]

The scientific community is in limbo. As technology keeps improving at rates that are ungraspable, our expertise seem to become a commodity purchasable at $4.00 per a million tokens. This is especially the case in the computer science community, as coding (and nowadays even programming and system design) seems close to solved. [^1] That leaves us with a question: What makes you a scientist? I would argue a good scientist most important feat is coming up with novel ideas, and be able to execute and communicate these ideas well. Now that more and more of the execution part is being partly outsourced, is all we have left being an idea machine? 

## Contemporary Artists

This new shift reminded me a lot of the process most contemporary artist now follow.  Some 'outsource' their work explicitly, like Marina Abramovic's performance art, which is often performed by hired actors once she has done the premiere. Walking through the entrance of a galery with Marina and her (then) husband Ulay must be different than my experience walking between two strangers in Kunsthaus. 

Others are not as explicit in their work though. This does not mean that they try to hide this, but it is not as apparant as not seeing the performance artists themselves be replaced by a stranger. When on a trip to Manchester I saw one of the largest expositions of Ai WeiWei's work, a giant rubber boat with refugees filling the gigantic hall. This was not the only huge work on display, and while he is the ideator, he did not create any of the pieces exhibited. 

This is how most contemporary artists operate now, within huge studios that help or sometimes completely take over the execution part of the process of creating an piece of art. From Yayoi Kusama (love), to Jeff Koons (hate), to Damien Hirst (???), they all have their own labs filled with students working on their creations. Although I've learned today that this is not necasserily a modern tradition, and that artists like Rembrandt (hup holland hup) had their students [paint in their name ](https://www.nga.gov/research/publications/dutch-paintings-seventeenth-century/issues-attribution-rembrandt-workshop)too. Every day is a school day!

## Contemporary Scientists

"Outsourcing" the work use to be the professors job, who would tackle the big problems while having their PhD students work on the smaller parts that make up the solution.  My professor used to have this [story of the rabbit and the lion](https://mfleck.cs.illinois.edu/parable.html) up on his website which explained this system perfectly. The professor thinks of the clockwork, while the students figure out the intricacies of the specific gears required to make the movement work just a tad bit more efficiently.

But this still pertained to just execution, and would leave ideas to be still a responsibility of the student. Some might argue that even ideation can be executed better by a sophisticated probability calculator too. At the start of this year, my professor send around [an article ](https://www.behind-the-enemy-lines.com/2026/02/everybody-is-ceo-now-and-what-exactly.html) by Panos Ipeirotis, a business professor who seemed to be an AI devotee, and was convinced that Claude was the best student he had supervised in the last 20 years:
>"I have never worked with a PhD student this organized and this diligent."

This idea is becoming more and more true though, and I tend to see a lot of people (Linkedin though, so take it with a huge block of salt) automate huge parts of their workflow. This also what is argued in ["When Ideas become Cheap"](https://jacktan.bearblog.dev/when-ideas-became-cheap/), which claims the researchers is not even ideation, but the only part that is left is judgement. With execution solved and ideation becoming better by the day, being able to separate the wheat from the chaff[^2] is where your value is at. That is what the contemporary scientist should focus on. 

## What Separates the Art from the Kitsch

How are scientists and artists similar? I would argue that both are at the core idea makers, and that modern times care less about your own capability to execute. This does not mean execution is becoming less important, as I think the opposite is true. It just means that you don't have to be the best coder in the world anymore, you just have to be able to judge it correctly. When everyone is able to create 'art', or 'do research', you have to be able to separate the [Bored Apes](https://opensea.io/collection/boredapeyachtclub) from the Matthew Wongs. The same thing holds for research. We just have to figure out what the kitsch is, and what qualifies as art. 

When looking into this idea a bit more, I stumbled on a post from Dan Cohen, which asks the same question: " [Is science becoming conceptual art?](https://newsletter.dancohen.org/archive/humane-ingenuity-52-is-science-becoming-conceptual-art/)"[^5]. It argues for the same future role of a scientist, although his work is related to a wet lab. Having a fully automated labratorium is still in the future (although Anthropic is I think working on something like this?), but this is already very much happening in CS labs. 

In present times we PhD students take on this part of the professor role as well, as execution does not seem to be our primary goal anymore. But when judgement should become our biggest strength, how do we train for this? Currently the system seems to run on the same assumptions as before, that a paper is worth sending in if it tests a couple of hypothesis in a seemingly sound manner. The recent [ICML](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-00893-2) and [CHI](https://chi2027.acm.org/2026/10/23/chi-2027-papers-desk-rejects-and-adr/) desk rejects show that a tsunami of automated papers and reviews is already here. 

## Are we doomed?

I am not so pessimistic in this regard. This technology provides the ability to iterate and experiment with your ideas quicker. Every thought that pops into your mind when you read a new paper, listen to a presentation or just on a walk through the woods would be written down, often to never be looked at again. Nowadays I can take these notes, prototype the idea, and see whether its worth diving deeper. 

In a time where execution and ideation is cheap, we shouldn't try to accelerate the rate of *publishing* all of our ideas, but rather the rate of *testing* all of our ideas. Figuring out which questions are even worth asking would not only allow us to focus on things that are interesting, but would also be beneficial for the rest of the scientific community who currently has to spit through thousands of shit papers, or 'Paper Diarrhea' as [coined by the head of ACM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ddCQfDMmGVg) himself.
